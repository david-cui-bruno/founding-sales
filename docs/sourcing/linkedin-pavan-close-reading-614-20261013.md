## Comparison: drafting decisions, not personality labels

The maps show three different mechanisms; do not put them all through the same short-post mold.

| Form | Attention contract | What a new draft must develop | Best ending decision |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Narrative | Explain a consequential change | Scene, pressure, choice, consequence, meaning | Return to the opening with changed understanding |
| Practical list | Make a problem usable | Each item needs a distinct mechanism and a concrete instance | Synthesize the rule or bounded next action |
| Announcement | Explain what is becoming available | What, when, for whom and access conditions | One relevant action and/or acknowledgment |
| Origin-story announcement | Explain how an outcome emerged | An obstacle sequence, not merely celebratory adjectives | Return to an earlier uncertain moment or state a bounded next step |

Editorial inference: spacing matters when it controls the **order of thinking**. A short hinge can suspend an unanswered question; a longer block can supply the evidence needed to earn the next hinge. Repeated blank lines without changing paragraph jobs only decorate a summary.

The useful distinction is not "candid versus corporate." It is whether the reader receives a specific situation, an actual choice, a complication, a mechanism and an earned conclusion. A hook may reveal the result while delaying the explanation. That is different from hiding the result, and different again from a numbered list that pays off item by item.

## Revised drafting checklist for this user's work

Everything in this section is an **editor recommendation**, not a claim about LinkedIn limits, ranking, best-performing length or Pavan's typical output.

### 1. Select material before selecting a hook

- Prioritize real founder/sales work and real technical work. A sales conversation, a messaging decision, a debugging investigation or a design tradeoff must come from supplied facts, not an invented autobiography.
- Use maintenance only when there is an actual insight: a surprising failure mode, a changed assumption, a customer consequence or a reusable decision rule. A routine task-completion update is not automatically a story.
- Build a fact ledger: what happened, what was observed, what was tried, what changed, what remains unknown, and what the user actually believes. Mark interpretation separately from result.
- If the facts cannot support the middle, request the missing scene or evidence during drafting. Do not pad with generic motivation to simulate depth.

### 2. Make the draft genuinely full-length

- For this calibration, target **300-450 words for a developed narrative or technical/founder essay**, with roughly **12-20 meaningful blank-separated blocks**. These are contextual working ranges, not quotas or platform facts.
- For a full-length practical list, provisionally target **250-350 words**, allowing each of three items a mechanism, real example and boundary. Do not stop after three compressed slogans.
- A real announcement can be shorter, but it should not be submitted as satisfying the requested long-form calibration. Do not invent a launch to obtain an easy format.
- Count the actual completed draft. If it is substantially below its selected working range, either develop missing beats using real material or explicitly explain why the evidence supports a shorter piece. Do not quietly return another short paragraph.
- Do not force a sparse factual packet to reach 450 words. Length is an acceptance aid for this task; earned development is the actual objective.

### 3. Allocate paragraph jobs before polishing voice

For a narrative/technical essay, sketch this sequence as jobs, not canned prose:

1. One bounded tension or consequence grounded in the user's work.
2. A reason the reader should care, without a universal promise.
3. The specific situation and relevant constraint.
4. The initial assumption or approach.
5. The observation that complicated it.
6. The practical stakes of that complication.
7. The actual decision or experiment.
8. What the evidence showed, including a failed or inconclusive result if relevant.
9. A re-hook that introduces a genuinely new distinction.
10. The mechanism explaining the result, not merely a lesson label.
11. The boundary: where the lesson might not apply.
12. A changed understanding that answers the opening.
13. An optional next step or acknowledgment only if warranted.

This is a flexible planning scaffold. Reorder or combine jobs when the factual sequence requires it. Avoid converting it into thirteen interchangeable motivational sentences.

### 4. Earn re-hooks and payoffs

- Put the first real example before the draft becomes an abstract essay. Prefer an actual objection, failed query, interface constraint or competing implementation over an unnamed "challenge."
- Re-hook at the point where the interpretation changes: a surprising observation, cost, constraint or distinction. Do not keep repeating the opening promise.
- Delay the causal explanation only when the intervening material adds necessary context. Do not withhold information merely to imitate suspense.
- Use transitions that express the relationship: cause, contradiction, chronology, correction or scope change. A row of isolated assertions is not a beat progression.
- For a list, each item must solve a different part of the problem. Include a concrete instance and what would change the advice. Numbering alone does not establish depth.

### 5. Use spacing as pacing

- Preserve blank lines between meaningful thought units. Mix short hinges with developed evidence blocks rather than putting every sentence on its own line.
- Reserve isolated short blocks for a genuine decision, reversal or conclusion. Their effect disappears if every block is equally emphatic.
- Keep examples readable enough to follow the actual mechanism. A technical draft must not discard the technical substance to look conversational.
- Inspect the finished draft as a whole: does the eye find distinct phases, and does every separated block earn its pause? Visual verification belongs to the later draft review, not to an invented claim about this research.

### 6. Truth and ending gates

- Do not borrow YC/Cisco history, Pavan's setbacks, group-chat details, photos, gratitude recipients or business outcomes. Borrow structural roles only.
- Do not add quantified results, customer quotes, experiences, positions or opinions unless supplied and attributable. A recommendation is not the user's past action.
- Do not convert an observation into a performance guarantee or a universal entrepreneurial belief. Keep uncertainty and limits visible.
- Do not force every post into a product pitch. A technical explanation can end at the mechanism; a sales reflection can end at the decision rule; a story can return to its opening.
- Add a CTA only when it follows from the specific topic and actual offering. Avoid unprovided free offers, comment-keyword funnels and unsupported launch promises.

- [ ] A complete opening, developed middle and intentional ending exist in each post.
- [ ] Actual word count and selected working range are stated, not inferred from screen height.
- [ ] The draft has a paragraph-job map; its middle cannot be summarized as "more encouragement."
- [ ] At least one concrete, user-grounded instance carries the argument.
- [ ] The re-hook changes understanding rather than restating the hook.
- [ ] The payoff answers the opening with evidence or a bounded insight.
- [ ] Spacing survives in the delivered draft, not just in a style description.
- [ ] Founder/sales or technical substance remains central; maintenance earns its inclusion.
